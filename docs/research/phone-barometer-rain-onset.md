## 1. WMO definition of pressure tendency

- Pressure tendency is "the character and amount of atmospheric pressure change for a three-hour or other specified period ending at the time of observation". It has two parts: the pressure change (net difference between readings at the beginning and end of the interval) and the pressure characteristic (how the pressure changed over the period, for example decreasing then increasing). WMO-No. 8, Vol. I, Ch. 3, Section 3.1.1 (2018 preliminary chapter, old.wmo.int/.../8_I_3_en_MR_clean.pdf).
- Section 3.5.1 of the same chapter: at surface synoptic stations the tendency and its characteristic "should be derived from pressure observations from the last 3 h (over 24 h in tropical regions)". With hourly observations the characteristic rests on four observations and misinterpretation may result, so a higher observation frequency, for example 10 minute intervals, is recommended. Nine characteristic types are defined (referring to WMO, 2011, the Manual on Codes).
- The characteristic is a code table. WMO registry 0-10-063 (status "stable", publisher WMO) lists: 0 increasing then decreasing, pressure same or higher than three hours ago; 1 increasing then steady, or increasing then increasing more slowly; 2 increasing (steadily or unsteadily); 3 decreasing or steady then increasing, or increasing then increasing more rapidly; 4 steady, pressure same as three hours ago; 5 decreasing then increasing, pressure same or lower than three hours ago; 6 decreasing then steady, or decreasing then decreasing more slowly; 7 decreasing (steadily or unsteadily); 8 steady or increasing then decreasing, or decreasing then decreasing more rapidly; 15 missing value (https://codes.wmo.int/bufr4/codeflag/0-10-063; summarised by a fetch tool, not verbatim).
- Consequence for the prototype: the WMO tendency is a 3 hour quantity (a signed change plus a shape class). A different window, for example 15 minutes or 1 hour, is a DataMend choice, not a WMO tendency, and should be named differently (UNVERIFIED that any WMO text defines a shorter standard window; none was found).
- WMO barometer uncertainty target: the Guide's measurement requirement for pressure is 0.1 hPa required target uncertainty with 0.1 hPa reporting resolution. Source: a search-result extract of the Guide's pressure requirements (WMO-No. 8 preliminary chapter family); I did not confirm this in the opened chapter text, though the chapter repeatedly refers to 0.1 hPa readings and corrections (for example "A good aneroid barometer should retain an accuracy of 0.1 hPa over a period ..." in the opened text). Treat the "required target uncertainty 0.1 hPa" row as UNVERIFIED against the current edition.

## 2. Pressure tendency as a rain signal

- Physical reasoning: Banerji and Kundu (1961), "Role of Pressure Tendencies in Forecasting Rainfall", MAUSAM 12(3), 475-480, argue that the pressure change at a place results from convergence, vertical transport and horizontal advection, and examine short-duration pressure tendencies in monsoon depressions where rainfall is mainly attributed to convergence. The abstract gives no skill figures or thresholds (https://mausamjournal.imd.gov.in/index.php/MAUSAM/article/view/4219; abstract only, paraphrased by a fetch tool). This is a 1961 India-region paper from the national meteorological service journal; it is relevant to the Mumbai setting but its numbers were not read.
- Rain precursors at one Swiss station (Remote Sensing 14(12), 2938, 2022, https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/14/12/2938): a search snippet reports temperature peaking 30 to 60 minutes before rain while pressure and relative humidity are at a minimum, and air density falling 2 to 6 hours before onset in 60 percent of events. UNVERIFIED: the paper could not be opened; the figures are from a search result snippet only. It also concerns a Swiss-plateau radiometer plus station dataset, not a phone sensor and not a monsoon climate.
- No primary source was found that gives a pressure-tendency threshold and its false-alarm rate for rain onset. Anything the app says about "rain soon" from tendency would therefore have no cited baseline; see section 5.

## 3. Smartphone barometer studies

- Mass and Madaus (2014), "Surface Pressure Observations from Smartphones: A Potential Revolution for High-Resolution Weather Prediction?", BAMS 95(9), 1343-1349, DOI 10.1175/BAMS-D-13-00188.1. Abstract (Crossref record, paraphrased): many smartphones contain fairly accurate pressure sensors, a count expected to reach hundreds of millions; the paper reviews the sensor technology and the companies collecting data, and includes an example of assimilating smartphone pressure during a strong convective storm over eastern Washington State. Purpose is numerical weather prediction, not per-device rain prediction. Full text not read.
- The companion AMS meeting abstract says surface pressure has smaller representativeness error than wind or temperature and that its biases are "usually static" and easy to remove (https://ams.confex.com/ams/94Annual/webprogram/Paper234541.html; paraphrased by a fetch tool).
- Hintz, Vedel and Kaas (2019), Meteorological Applications 26(4), 733-746. Statements read in the thesis reprint:
  - Abstract-level: results of earlier studies, "a substantial but long-term stable bias in combination with high relative accuracy", are confirmed; over 6 million observations in seven weeks from Denmark; the screening reduces noise but discards most observations; in one case study assimilation reduced surface pressure bias without raising RMSE; the altitude assigned to phones needs improvement. (Abstract as summarised by a fetch tool from https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/da/publications/collecting-and-processing-of-barometric-data-from-smartphones-for/.)
  - Section 2 (controlled test): two phones, an Apple iPhone 6 (Bosch BMP280 sensor per the paper) and a Samsung Galaxy A5, were compared with a Vaisala PTB330 reference (absolute accuracy 0.2 hPa) at the same height in a Danish Meteorological Institute facility over seven sessions in one month. Bias was 0.96 +/- 0.06 hPa (iPhone 6) and -2.06 +/- 0.09 hPa (Galaxy A5). After downsampling to the reference's 10 minute interval, Pearson correlation with the reference was 0.977 (iPhone 6) and 0.965 (Galaxy A5). The two phones' 1 Hz series correlated at 0.994, with short-term variability the reference did not resolve.
  - Spin-up: the short-term variance of a phone at rest is larger at the start of a measurement; an average spin-up of about 5 s was found (50 series of 180 s), attributed to a sensor-internal infinite impulse response filter (citing McNicholas and Mass, 2018); the standard deviation over all experiments was 0.02 hPa.
  - Conclusion: relative phone pressure is reliable, while biases of the order of 1 hPa exist and were found stable over the test period (the sentence is cut by the column layout in my text extraction; the exact length of "stable over periods of ..." is UNVERIFIED).
  - Screening: 80 percent of observations were flagged by a background check, and 88.6 and 90.6 percent were flagged in two later setups (thesis reprint, Section 5 text). Context in the same text: "1 hPa corresponds to approximately 8 m altitude difference" and NWP surface-pressure standard deviation of the order of 0.3 to 0.4 hPa.
  - Scale of the study: two phones in the lab test, so the bias values describe those two devices only; they are not a population statistic.
- Hintz et al. also cite earlier phone work (Kim et al. 2015, 2016; McNicholas and Mass 2018, bias correction with machine learning; Madaus and Mass 2017). These were not opened.

## 4. Sensor noise, drift, altitude, and Android access

- Android sensor interface: the pressure sensor (`TYPE_PRESSURE`) reports one value in hPa or mbar, "ambient air pressure". The page states that environment sensors "do not typically require any data filtering", that they exist only if the manufacturer built them in, and that an app must check at runtime whether the sensor exists (https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/sensors/sensors_environment). The docs do not state sensor noise, accuracy or drift for Android as a platform, only the unit.
- Sensor part example: Bosch BMP390 (a phone-class barometer; which sensor a given Android phone contains is device-specific and UNVERIFIED for the prototype phone). Product page typical values: relative accuracy +/-0.03 hPa (700 to 1100 hPa, 25 to 40 C); absolute accuracy +/-0.50 hPa (300 to 1100 hPa, 0 to 65 C); temperature coefficient offset +/-0.6 Pa/K (25 to 40 C, 900 hPa); long-term stability +/-0.16 hPa over 12 months; solder drift below +/-0.8 hPa; noise 0.02 Pa RMS at the lowest bandwidth and highest resolution; stated use "altitude tracking applications" (https://www.bosch-sensortec.com/products/environmental-sensors/pressure-sensors/bmp390/). The BMP280 figure quoted by Hintz et al. is 1 hPa absolute and 0.12 hPa relative accuracy (Bosch Sensortech, 2018, as cited in the paper). The datasheet itself was not opened.
- WMO on drift: drift between calibrations is "one of the key sources of error with barometers", often greater when new, and step jumps may occur; electronic barometers need frequent checks, "starting annually" (WMO-No. 8 Vol. I Ch. 3, Section 3.2.7.1, 2018 preliminary). Phone sensors are never recalibrated by the app, so slow drift and steps are a documented risk for any tendency computed over long windows; windows of minutes to hours are far shorter than the 12 month figure above (that comparison is my reading, not a published result).
- Altitude and elevation effects: a 1 hPa change corresponds to roughly 8 m of height (Hintz et al., above). A phone carried between floors or moved in a lift therefore produces a pressure step unrelated to weather. Android's `SensorManager.getAltitude(p0, p)` converts pressure to metres relative to a sea-level pressure p0 and can compute height differences using `PRESSURE_STANDARD_ATMOSPHERE` (1013.25 hPa) when p0 is unknown ("absolute altitudes won't be accurate"; https://developer.android.com/reference/android/hardware/SensorManager). Hintz et al. state that the altitude assigned to phones needs improvement for NWP use. For a single static device the absolute altitude does not matter for a tendency, but a height change between samples does.
- Temperature and handling effects on a phone (pocket heat, charging) are not covered by any source opened; UNVERIFIED. The BMP390 TCO figure above is the only quantified temperature dependence found.
- Background access (relevant to the periodic-training design): "Android 9 limits the ability for background apps to access user input and sensor data". In the background, sensors using continuous, on-change or one-shot reporting modes do not receive events; the guidance is to use a foreground service (https://developer.android.com/about/versions/pie/android-9.0-changes-all). The pressure sensor's reporting mode was not checked; the same page implies on-change and continuous are both blocked, so a background job cannot assume it can read the barometer. Behaviour on Android 10 and later was NOT checked here; UNVERIFIED and should be confirmed before the sensor-collection slice.
- Sampling: Hintz et al. note the iOS API returned 1 Hz while "the Android operating system can return higher sampling rates" and the BMP280's average measurement interval is 5.5 ms. Android's `registerListener` delivery rate is app-chosen (see the Android examples using `SENSOR_DELAY_NORMAL`); the rate actually delivered for a given phone was not measured.

## 5. What skill is realistic

- Published measured skill for single-device rain-onset prediction from pressure tendency: NOT FOUND in primary sources. The closest items are the precursor-timing snippet in section 2 (UNVERIFIED) and the phone-pressure-for-NWP studies in section 3, which test pressure data as model input, not a per-device rain classifier. The only precipitation result seen is that assimilating phone pressure in one case "improved accumulated precipitation skill" (Hintz et al. abstract, via the KU record, abstract-level and a single case), which says nothing about per-device onset prediction.
- What the sources do support: phone pressure has high relative accuracy and stable device bias (Hintz et al.), so a short-window change in pressure from one device is measurable at the ~0.1 hPa scale; the tendency is a weather-scale quantity (WMO 3 h definition). They do not show that a tendency threshold separates "rain in the next N minutes" from "no rain" at an acceptable false-alarm rate.
- Per AGENTS.md section 4 and 9: any skill statement for the prototype must come from its own evaluation (precision, recall, F1, false-alarm rate per 1,000 clean items, detection delay). No baseline number from the literature can be cited for it. A sensible reference baseline for such an evaluation (persistence of "no rain", or a plain threshold on 3 h change) is a DataMend design choice, not something these sources prescribe.

## 6. Label sources for tap-style reports

- mPING (NOAA NSSL, deployed 2012): a free smartphone app where anyone can submit a weather observation anonymously; reports go to an NSSL database and a public map; used to check radar precipitation-type algorithms (rain, hail, snow, freezing rain, ice pellets) and by forecast offices (NSSL fact sheet, March 2014). Elmore et al. (2014), BAMS 95(9), DOI 10.1175/BAMS-D-13-00014.1, abstract-level via a search result only: winter surface precipitation type reports are "at least equivalent in quality" to human-augmented ASOS observations, and later work notes mPING is biased toward freezing rain and ice pellets relative to augmented ASOS (secondary, UNVERIFIED). Relevance: this shows a crowd report is usable as a verification label for precipitation type at a point and time; it does NOT address timing accuracy of "it just started raining" taps, and the data are US-centred.
- Tap timing noise: NOT FOUND. No primary source was opened that quantifies the delay between actual rain onset and a user's tap, or the rate of forgotten or mistaken taps. For a one-user prototype this is the main label risk and has to be measured by the prototype (for example by comparing taps against the nearest platform gauge feed), not assumed.
- Gauge labels as an alternative or cross-check: the platform's own Weather Union Mumbai gauge feed is a project source, not covered by this research (units UNCONFIRMED per `MAP.md`); a gauge is not at the phone's location, so it cannot be a point label without a distance and time tolerance. This is a design question for the model/labels ticket, not a sourced finding.
- Label values that follow from the sources: a tap is a human report with unknown delay; if used, it should be stored raw and separately from any derived label (AGENTS.md section 6, raw data never overwritten).
